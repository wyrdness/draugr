## Overview

The draugr (Old Norse draugr, plural draugar) is a corporeal undead being of Old Norse, Icelandic, Norwegian, and Faroese tradition. Unlike incorporeal ghosts, draugar are physical, often described as 'death-blue' (hel-blár) or 'corpse-pale' (nár-fölr), bloated to the size of bulls or oxen, reeking of decay, and possessed of immense strength. Old Norse literature distinguishes the haugbúi or 'mound-dweller', who guards a barrow and attacks only those who trespass, from the aptrganga or 'after-walker', who leaves the grave to terrorize a wider community. Draugar wield trollskapr — a magical capacity that includes shape-shifting (into seals, bulls, cats, or half-flayed monstrosities), changing size, controlling weather, entering the dreams of the living, sinking through solid ground, and inflicting curses. Famous saga draugar include Glámr the shepherd in Grettis saga, Kárr inn gamli (Kar the Old) the mound-guardian in Grettis saga, Víga-Hrappr Sumarliðason in Laxdœla saga, and Þórólfr bægifótr (Thorolf Lame-foot) in Eyrbyggja saga. Methods recorded in the sagas for ending a draugr include wrestling, decapitation, burning, and casting the ashes into the sea. The Norwegian sjødraug ('sea-draug') is a related figure: the ghost of a drowned fisherman without Christian burial, often seen sailing a half-boat as a death-omen.

## Etymology

- **Origin**: Old Norse draugr, from Proto-Germanic *draugaz ('phantom, illusion'), Indo-European *dʰrowgʰos
- **Meaning**: Linked to a root meaning 'illusion, deceit'; cognate with German Trug ('deception') and Sanskrit drúh ('injury, harm')
- **First use**: Old Norse poetry and prose; defined by Cleasby & Vigfússon (1873) as 'a ghost, spirit, especially the dead inhabitant of a cairn'

## Theories & Explanations

### Paranormal / Cryptozoological

**Saga revenant tradition** _(Saga authors and audiences (medieval Iceland))_

Within the worldview of the medieval Icelandic sagas, draugar are treated as real corporeal returning dead governed by their character in life.

### Skeptical

**Literary and folkloric reading** _(Ármann Jakobsson, modern Old Norse studies)_

Modern scholarship (e.g., Ármann Jakobsson) treats draugar as cultural figures that articulate medieval Norse anxieties about death, social marginality, property, and contagion rather than as historical entities.

**Misinterpretation of decomposition** _(Modern forensic and folkloric scholarship)_

Saga descriptions of bloating, blackening, and unnatural posture in draugar are consistent with normal post-mortem changes interpreted in pre-modern terms.
